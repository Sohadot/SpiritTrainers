# NAMING_POWER.md

> Why names — not products, funding, or features — are what create categories.
> This document is about naming in general. It mentions "Spirit Trainers" only at
> the end, as an instance of a principle argued on its own terms first. If the
> principle is wrong, the asset cannot lean on it; so the principle is examined
> before it is applied.

---

## 1. A category is a word before it is a market

We tend to think markets come first and names label them afterward. Often it is
the reverse. A diffuse set of activities stays invisible as a *category* until a
word gathers them into one. Before the word, there are only instances. After the
word, there is a *thing* — something people can point at, compare themselves to,
belong to, or refuse.

The word does not describe the category. The word **constitutes** it. This is the
core of naming power: a name is not a label placed on a pre-existing box; the
name is what makes the box exist.

## 2. What a category name does that a product cannot

A product competes *inside* a frame. A category name *sets* the frame. The
difference in leverage is enormous:

- A product asks, "choose me over my rivals."
- A category name asks, "understand this whole space through me."

The product that wins a frame can be displaced by a better product. The name that
*is* the frame is displaced only by re-founding the whole category — which almost
never happens. This is why the most durable positions in any market are held not
by the best product but by whoever owns the word.

## 3. The mechanics of naming power

A category name accrues power through four compounding effects:

1. **Default retrieval.** When people need to refer to the space, they reach for
   the name. Each retrieval reinforces the name as the space's identity.
2. **Relative positioning.** Everyone else must define themselves *relative* to
   the name ("like X, but for…"). The name becomes the coordinate system.
3. **Membership.** Practitioners begin to describe themselves using the name,
   importing its meaning into their own identity — for free, at scale.
4. **Compression.** The name lets a complex space be invoked in two words,
   lowering the cost of thinking about it. Cheaper-to-think-about ideas spread
   further.

None of these depend on a product existing. They depend only on the name being
clean, well-formed, and adopted.

## 4. Why generic names rarely lead categories

There is a paradox worth stating: the most *descriptive* names are often the
*weakest* category names. "Human Potential Solutions" describes everything and
therefore compresses nothing; it cannot become a default because it is
indistinguishable from a definition. A category name must be specific enough to be
*a name* and general enough to *hold a category* — a narrow band that most
candidate phrases miss on one side or the other. (This is developed for the buyer
in `BRAND_ARCHITECTURE.md`.)

## 5. Why the naming window closes

Category names are subject to a quiet race. The space stays unnamed only until
someone notices the gap and moves. Once a name is adopted — by an incumbent, a
challenger, or simply by usage — the window is closed, often for a generation. The
value of noticing an unnamed category early is precisely the value of acting
before the window closes.

This is not urgency theatre; it is the ordinary dynamics of language. Words settle.
Once "the word for that" exists, alternatives become uphill fights against an
established default.

## 6. The application (stated once, at the end)

Everything above is general. Its bearing on this asset is a single sentence:

> If names constitute categories, then a clean, well-formed, currently-unadopted
> name for an existing cross-disciplinary practice is not a domain — it is a
> latent category, and owning it is owning the frame rather than competing inside
> one.

*Spirit Trainers* is offered as exactly such a name. The rest of the asset argues
that it is clean, well-formed, and unadopted (`SEMANTIC_POSITION.md`,
`CATEGORY_EVIDENCE.md`); this document argues only that if those are true, the
name is worth more than a name usually is.
