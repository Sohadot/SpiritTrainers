Why Categories Need Names

On names & categories Spirit Trainers

It is tempting to believe that a category is held together by its substance — that surgeons form a category because of surgery, or that architects form one because of buildings. The substance seems so obviously prior to the word that the word looks like an afterthought, a convenient label stuck on a group that already existed.

But watch what happens to an activity that has no name. It does not form a category. It forms a scatter. The people doing it do not recognize each other. They read different journals, attend different conferences, answer to different titles, and compete in different markets — not because their work differs, but because nothing has told them their work is the same work. Without a shared word, there is no shared mirror. Each practitioner sees only their own corner and assumes the corner is the whole room.

A name is the first thing that lets them see the room.

This is the quiet, underrated function of a category name: it is an act of gathering. Before the word, there are instances — a mentor here, a drill instructor there, a performance coach somewhere else, a dean of character in a school no one thinks to compare to a military academy. After the word, there is a category, and the instances become members of it. Nothing about the underlying work changed. What changed is that a word made the pattern visible, and a visible pattern is a thing people can join, study, fund, and defend.

Names also do a second, stranger thing: they make a category *thinkable at scale*. A phrase compresses a complicated space into something a person can hold in one thought and pass to another person in one breath. "Athletic training" lets you refer, instantly, to an entire profession you could otherwise only gesture at. Compression is not decoration; it is what lets an idea travel. Ideas that cannot be said in a few words do not spread, because every transmission costs too much. A category without a name is an idea that is expensive to think — and expensive ideas stay small.

There is a test for whether a name has done its work. Ask whether people define themselves by it and against it. The moment practitioners say "I am one of those" and competitors say "we are like that, but different," the name has stopped describing the category and started constituting it. It has become the coordinate system the whole space is measured in. That is the endpoint every category name is quietly reaching for, and most never arrive, because most names are either too vague to mean anything or too owned to be shared.

Which returns us to the uncomfortable fact underneath all of this. If names gather scattered practice into categories, then a real, widespread practice that has no canonical name is not a category that failed to form. It is a category waiting for its word. The practitioners are already there. The work is already being done. The only thing missing is the thing that would let them see each other — and whoever supplies that word supplies the mirror the whole category will, from then on, look into.
