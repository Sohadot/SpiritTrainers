Acquisition

Not a domain. A category.

The same asset can be valued two ways. This one is framed as the strategic one.

Two questions, two prices

A domain sale asks

"What is this URL worth to you today?"

A category acquisition asks

"What is it worth to own the name of a market before it consolidates — and what does it cost if someone else owns it first?"

What the acquisition includes

A coherent bundle, not a bare name.

  • The canonical nameSpiritTrainers.com — the clean top-level identity
  • The thesisa public, reasoned case that the name is a category — inherited, already made
  • The meaninga fixed, defensible definition that protects the name from drift
  • The positioningthe map of every market the name can enter
  • The optionalitythe right to activate it as a brand, institute, certification, or hold

A domain sale transfers only the first. This transfers all five. The difference is the thesis.

The cost of inaction

The name is either an asset on your balance sheet, or a headline on a competitor's. There is no third state where it stays neutral forever.

Clean category names do not stay available. Ownership is less an opportunity to seize than a position to secure before it is taken.

What is not claimed

Honesty is the pricing strategy.

This asset does not promise revenue, traffic, or growth. It does not claim the category is already established — only that the name for it is available and inevitable. It does not guarantee the buyer's outcome. It transfers a position. Activating it is the buyer's work. A precise, self-limiting case is harder to argue with — and that is exactly what commands a strategic price.
