# BUYER_LOGIC.md

> Who could acquire this category, why they would, and what they are actually
> buying. This document is written for the seller's clarity first; its arguments
> are then translated into the public `content/buyer-fit.md` and the site's
> "Who Could Hold This Category Position?" section.

---

## 1. What the buyer is really buying

The buyer is **not** buying a domain flip, traffic, or a codebase. They are
buying **three things a strategic acquisition secures that a domain sale does
not:**

1. **Category positioning leverage.** The party that holds the exact-match name
   and the public case for it starts from the strongest position to become the
   market's default — the point every competitor then has to name themselves
   *relative to*. It is leverage, not a guarantee: the position still has to be
   activated.
2. **Activation optionality.** The name can be activated as a brand, a division,
   an institute, a methodology, a media property, or held defensively — subject
   to the buyer's own clearance. The buyer purchases a set of futures, not one
   product.
3. **Exact-domain exclusivity.** While the buyer holds `SpiritTrainers.com`, no
   rival holds that exact address. This is exclusivity over the canonical
   *identity*, not over the two words themselves (`CLAIM_BOUNDARY.md`).

## 2. Buyer archetypes

Each archetype sees a *different* value in the same name. That multiplicity is
what makes the asset liquid.

### A. Coaching & development platforms
- **Why they buy:** they already operate in the category but under a generic or
  founder-named brand. "Spirit Trainers" gives them the umbrella name.
- **What they fear losing:** a competitor consolidating the category above them.

### B. Corporate wellbeing & resilience companies
- **Why they buy:** "wellbeing" is soft and crowded; "Spirit Trainers" reframes
  their work as *training* — methodical, serious, outcome-oriented.
- **What they fear losing:** being commoditized as a perk vendor.

### C. Leadership institutes & universities
- **Why they buy:** an institutional, non-sectarian name for a center on
  character, judgment, and human development.
- **What they fear losing:** relevance to a generation that wants substance
  without dogma.

### D. Military, first-responder & readiness programs
- **Why they buy:** "resilience training" is already their language; this is its
  canonical civilian-compatible name.
- **What they fear losing:** nothing — but the name dignifies and unifies work
  they already fund heavily.

### E. Sports & mental-performance brands
- **Why they buy:** "the inner game" needs a category name that isn't owned by
  one athlete or one method.
- **What they fear losing:** their edge to a rival that names the space first.

### F. EdTech & youth-development organizations
- **Why they buy:** "character education" is politically loaded; "Spirit
  Trainers" is warmer, broader, and future-facing.
- **What they fear losing:** the framing war over what schools are *for*.

## 3. The buyer's decision, in one line each

- The **strategic buyer** asks: *"If I don't own this, who does — and what does
  that cost me?"*
- The **brand buyer** asks: *"Could this become the name people say instead of
  the thing I currently call myself?"*
- The **defensive buyer** asks: *"Is this cheaper to own than to compete
  against?"*

The asset is built so that all three arrive at "yes."

## 4. Qualifying the buyer (seller-side)

Not every interested party is the right buyer. The right buyer:

- operates in, or adjacent to, one of the archetypes above,
- thinks in *positions and categories*, not in *domain flips*,
- has the standing to actually activate the name at institutional scale.

A buyer who only sees "a nice domain" will underprice the asset because they are
valuing the URL, not the category. The entire site exists to filter *for* the
strategic buyer and *against* the domain flipper.

## 5. Why the name is hard to replace

A prospective buyer will ask their team, "can't we just find another name?" The
honest answer — and the one the asset makes them feel — is:

- Short, positive, cross-cultural two-word category names are **structurally
  scarce**; you cannot brute-force one into existence.
- Every strong alternative is either already owned, too niche, too clinical, or
  too clever to become a default.
- The rarity is not marketing. It is the reason the name has value at all.

## 6. Handoff to public content

The public-facing translation of this logic lives in:
- `content/buyer-fit.md` (the "who could hold this position" narrative),
- `data/buyers.json` (the structured archetype list the site renders),
- the site's **Buyer Fit** page.

Nothing in the public content should reveal seller-side pricing strategy; it
should only make each archetype *recognize itself*.
