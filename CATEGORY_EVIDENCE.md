# CATEGORY_EVIDENCE.md

> The empirical support for the observation. This document catalogues real,
> already-accepted "___ Trainers" categories and then examines why "Spirit
> Trainers" reads as a natural member of the same series rather than an invented
> outlier. It is evidence, not argument — the argument lives in
> `CATEGORY_THESIS.md`.

---

## 1. The existing series

Each of the following is a phrase the market already treats as a *category*, not
merely a description. Each names a domain in which capability is deliberately
built.

| Category            | Domain of capability                     | Roughly since |
|---------------------|------------------------------------------|---------------|
| Athletic Trainers   | physical performance and injury          | early 20th c. |
| Personal Trainers   | individual physical conditioning         | late 20th c.  |
| Corporate Trainers  | workplace skills and process             | mid 20th c.   |
| Dog Trainers        | animal behavior                          | long-standing |
| Executive Trainers  | leadership conduct and judgment          | recent        |
| AI Trainers         | machine behavior and alignment           | 2020s         |

(Dates are indicative, not asserted as precise facts; they mark the pattern, not
a timeline. See `governance/QUALITY_GATE.md` on not fabricating figures.)

## 2. What the series has in common

Read the column of domains and a structure appears. Every accepted "___
Trainers" category shares four traits:

1. **A definable domain** — there is a *something* being trained (a body, a
   skill, an animal, a model).
2. **Deliberate method** — the training is intentional and repeatable, not
   incidental.
3. **Recognizable practitioners** — you can point at the people who do it.
4. **A phrase that needs no manual** — hearing "X Trainers," you infer roughly
   what X-training is.

These four traits are the membership conditions of the series. They are also the
test any new candidate must pass.

## 3. Where "Spirit Trainers" sits in the series

Apply the same four conditions to the human interior:

1. **A definable domain?** Yes — character, resilience, judgment, inner strength.
   Ordinary language already treats it as a *something*.
2. **Deliberate method?** Yes — it is trained the way any capability is, by graded
   difficulty and repetition. (Militaries, mentors, and coaches have done so for
   centuries.)
3. **Recognizable practitioners?** Yes — but scattered under borrowed labels
   (drill instructor, mentor, chaplain, performance coach, dean of character).
4. **A phrase that needs no manual?** Yes — "Spirit Trainers" is immediately
   roughly legible.

On all four conditions, "Spirit Trainers" behaves exactly like the accepted
members of the series. It is not an outlier being forced in; it is the member the
series is missing.

## 4. The one condition it fails — and why that is the point

There is a fifth trait the accepted categories share that "Spirit Trainers" does
*not*: **consolidation.** For each accepted category, the market has settled on
the phrase as the default name. For the human interior, it has not — the label is
still fragmented across fields.

This is precisely the observation restated as evidence: every membership
condition is met *except* the one that only time, or an owner, can supply. The
name is category-shaped in every respect but adoption.

## 5. Why it reads as natural, linguistically

Beyond the series logic, the phrase is phonetically and grammatically at home:

- It follows the exact `[Domain] + Trainers` construction of every item above.
- Both words are common, short, and require no gloss.
- The stress pattern matches the series (*Áthletic Tráiners*, *Spírit Tráiners*).

Nothing about the phrase signals novelty or invention. It sounds like a term you
half-remember already existing — which is the signature of a category name that
was available rather than manufactured. (The formal linguistic treatment is in
`SEMANTIC_POSITION.md`.)

## 6. Honest limits of the evidence

- The series comparison is *suggestive*, not *proof*. It shows the phrase is
  well-formed and well-positioned; it does not, by itself, guarantee adoption.
- "Already-accepted" is a judgment about common usage, not a measured claim. No
  usage statistics are asserted here.
- The evidence supports the *observation* and the *naturalness* of the name. The
  strategic value it implies is argued elsewhere, not smuggled in here.
